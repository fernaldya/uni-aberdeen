\section{Definition and Method}
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{w3-1.png}
    \caption{Cohort study design}
    \label{fig:w3-1}
\end{figure}

As the image above says, starting from choosing people \textbf{WITHOUT} the outcome of interest the study population then follow-up splitting them into either the exposed or not exposed group and ending up with the outcome or no outcome from both groups.
\\
Conducting a cohort study follows these steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Research question - Think about how exposure is associated with the outcome(s)
    \item Identify the study population - This depends on how common the exposure of interest is
    \item Measure the exposure
    \item Follow-up participants and determine their outcome status
    \item Data analysis
    \item Interpret the results
    \item Assess possible sources of error
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Measure the exposure}
Measuring the exposure can be gathered from a number of sources:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Medical records / tests
    \item Measures of blood
    \item Physical measurements, e.g. height, weight, etc.
    \item Direct questioning to the subject of interest, questionnaires
    \item Environmental measures, e.g. water sampling
\end{itemize}

It is highly important to define the exposure, such as \textit{how has epxosure been ascertained?} or \textit{how often has exposure been measured?} to avoid boundary (a maybe situation) cases. Confounding variables also need to be measured.

\subsection*{Follow up participants and determine their outcome status}
Just like defining the exposure, outcome also needs to be defined clearly and its ascertainment for the same reason to avoid boundary cases. Furthermore, things like \textit{do all the population have similar risk of outcome?} or whether the population at risk has changed over time needs to be thought out thoroughly.

\subsection*{Data analysis}
Cohort study calculates \textbf{incidence} as a risk or rate, which can then be compared to get the risk / rate ratio. The difference in calculating risk or rate is typically on whether the follow-up time is identical (or nearly) for the cohort members, risk is for when the follow-up time is identical, while rate is when there's variation between the follow-up time of the cohort members.
\\~\\
Analysis should also allow for the changing nature of exposure and for the differences in groups (by using standardisation, multivariate adjustments, Poisson regression, or Cox regression). One thing to note is \textbf{Effect modification}, it is when the effect of an exposure or treatment of an outcome changes depending on the level of a third variable, i.e. \textit{A third variable distorts the exposure, outcome, or both}

\subsubsection*{Calculating risk}
When the follow-up time is \textbf{identical} (or nearly) identical for all cohort members.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{w3-2.png}
    \caption{Calculate risk}
    \label{fig:w3-2}
\end{figure}
\\
The formulas for the risks are:
\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{2.0}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|X|X|}
\textbf{Risk in exposed}, $r_e = \dfrac{a}{a + b}$ & \textbf{Risk in unexposed}, $r_0 = \dfrac{c}{c + d}$ \\ 
\textbf{Risk ratio} = $\dfrac{r_e}{r_0}$             & \textbf{Risk difference} = $r_e - r_0$ \\ 
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Calculating risk in cohort studies}
\label{tab:risk-calculation}
\end{table}

\subsubsection*{Calculating rate}
When there is \textbf{variation} between cohort members in the duration of follow-up time.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{w3-3.png}
    \caption{Calculate rate}
    \label{fig:w3-3}

\\
The formulas for the rates are:
\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{2.0}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|X|X|}
\textbf{Rate in exposed}, $r_e = \dfrac{a}{y_e}$ & \textbf{Risk in unexposed}, $r_0 = \dfrac{c}{y_0}$ \\ 
\textbf{Risk ratio} = $\dfrac{r_e}{r_0}$             & \textbf{Risk difference} = $r_e - r_0$ \\ 
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Calculating rate in cohort studies}
\label{tab:rate-calculation}
\end{table}

\subsection*{Interpret results}
Interpreting the results follows these 3 principles:
\begin{itemize}
    \item RR = 1 -$>$ No association
    \item RR $>$ 1 -$>$ Risk in exposed is greater than risk in not exposed
    \item RR $<$ 1 -$>$ Risk in exposed is less than risk in not exposed
\end{itemize}
Where RR is the risk / rate ratio

\subsection*{Assess possible sources of error}
Errors in studies can come from multiple sources, but primarily they may come from bias, confounding variables, or even chance. Examples of bias are information bias or attrition bias that came due to cohort members not available for the follow-up. It is highly important to collect information about possible confounders.

\section{Types of Cohort Studies}
Cohort studies are divided into three based on how the participants are followed in time.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prospective} (Concurrent)
    \item \textbf{Retrospective} (Historical)
    \item \textbf{Ambidirectional}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Retrospective cohort study}
This study reconstructs past exposure history and track subsequent health outcome using existing records. The pros and cons are
\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|X|X|}
\hline
\textbf{Advantage} & \textbf{Disadvantage} \\ \hline
\textbf{Rare exposures} can be studied   & Inefficient for \textbf{rare disease} \\ \hline
Can examine multiple outcomes   & Differential loss to follow up can lead to attrition / information bias \\ \hline
Can provide information about the time course of outcome (natural history) & Exposure status can change \\ \hline
Can measure incidence rate directly & Can become historical \\ \hline
Temporal sequence of events can be established  & \textbf{Investigator has no control over how data was collected} \\ \hline

\end{tabularx}
\caption{Pros and cons of retrospective cohort study}
\label{tab:pros-cons-retrospective-cohort-study}
\end{table}

\subsection{Ambidirectional cohort study}
This study combines both elements from prospective and retrospective cohort study. It works by looking at past records to identify the cohort, determining their past exposures then follow them into the future to determine their health outcome.

\section{Exercise on calculating risk and rates in cohort studies}
\subsection{Example 1}
A cohort study began in 1990 and followed participants to determine
whether poor sleep quality was associated with risk of incident stroke. At
study recruitment, 9936 participants agreed to take part and completed
the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index. 8040 people had good sleep quality.
After 10 years of follow-up, 145 strokes had occurred in people with
good sleep quality and 87 strokes had occurred in people with poor sleep
quality.
\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|X|X|X|X|}
\hline
& \multicolumn{2}{c|}{\textbf{Outcome}} & \\ \cline{2-3}
\textbf{Exposure} & \textbf{Stroke} & \textbf{No Stroke} & \textbf{Total} \\ \hline
\textbf{Exposed - Poor Sleep} & 87 & 1809 & 1896 \\ \hline
\textbf{Not exposed - Good Sleep} & 145 & 7895 & 8040 \\ \hline
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Example 1}
\label{tab:example-1}
\end{table}

Since the follow-up is on the same time, the risk is calculated here instead of the rate.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Risk in exposed group, $r_e = \dfrac{87}{1896} = 0.046$
    \item Risk in not exposed group, $r_0 = \dfrac{145}{8040} = 0.018$
    \item Risk ratio = $\dfrac{0.046}{0.018} = 2.56$
\end{itemize}
In conclusion, people with poor sleep quality had 2.56 times the risk of stroke than people with good sleep quality.

\subsection{Example 2}
During 1995-2014, a cohort study followed 1,879,227 women aged 15
to 49 living in Denmark to investigate the relationship between use of
hormonal contraceptives and risk of ovarian cancer. Women were
followed until either: first ovarian cancer, death, emigration, age 50 or
end of follow-up. The total follow-up time in the study was 21,494,781
person-years, of which 13,344,531 person-years occurred in users of
hormonal contraceptives. 1249 ovarian cancers were registered, most
of which were in women who did not use hormonal contraceptives
(n=771 cancers).

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|X|X|X|}
\hline
& \multicolumn{2}{c|}{\textbf{Outcome}} \\ \cline{2-3}
\textbf{Exposure} & \textbf{Ovarian Cancer} & \textbf{Person-years} \\ \hline
\textbf{Exposed - HC user} & 478 & 13,344,531 \\ \hline
\textbf{Not exposed - HC non-user} & 771 & 8,150,250 \\ \hline
\textbf{Total} & 1249 & 21,494,781 \\ \hline
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Example 2}
\label{tab:example-2}
\end{table}

The rate is calculated here since the follow-up time varies between the cohort members.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rate in exposed group, $r_e = \dfrac{478}{1,334,4531} = 0.000035824$ or 3.58 per 100,000 person-years
    \item Rate in non-exposed group, $r_0 = \dfrac{771}{8,150,250} = 0.0000946$ or 9.46 per 100,000 person-years
    \item Rate ratio = $\dfrac{3.58 \text{ per 100,000 person-years}}{9.46 \text{ per 100,000 person-years}} \approx 0.38$
\end{itemize}
In conclusion, women who used hormonal contraceptives had 0.38 times the rate of ovarian cancer compared to non-users.

\subsection{More on Person-time}
For an individual, person-time accounts for the total time period during which an individual could develop or is at risk of the outcome of interest.
\\~\\
For a population / cohort study, total person-time at risk is the sum of all individuals person-time, the total time during which outcomes could occur and would be considered events in the population of interest.

\subsubsection{Example - Person-time}
A cohort study recruits from January 1995 and follows members until lung cancer or death from another cause or end of study in December 2014.
\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|X|X|X|X|}
\hline
\textbf{Exposure status} & \textbf{Date of entry} & \textbf{Event} & \textbf{Event date} \\ \hline
Smoker & Jan 1995 & Lung cancer & Mar 2006 \\ \hline
Non-Smoker & Feb 1995 & Death & June 2014 \\ \hline
Non-Smoker & Apr 1995 & End of study & Dec 2014 \\ \hline
Non-Smoker & Jan 1995 & Lung cancer & Aug 2000 \\ \hline
Smoker & Feb 1995 & Lung cancer & Jul 2002 \\ \hline
Smoker & Jun 1995 & End of study & Dec 2014 \\ \hline
Smoker & Mar 1995 & Death & Nov 2001 \\ \hline
Non-Smoker & Jan 1995 & End of study & Dec 2014 \\ \hline
Non-Smoker & Mar 1995 & End of study & Dec 2014 \\ \hline
Smoker & Jan 1995 & Lung cancer & Feb 2012 \\ \hline
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Person-time example}
\label{tab:example-person-time}
\end{table}
Calculate the person-years and the rates.
\\
\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|X|X|X|X|X|}
\hline
\textbf{Exposure status} & \textbf{Date of entry} & \textbf{Event} & \textbf{Event date} & \textbf{Person-years} \\ \hline
Smoker & Jan 1995 & Lung cancer & Mar 2006 & 11.25 \\ \hline
Non-Smoker & Feb 1995 & Death & June 2014 & 19.42 \\ \hline
Non-Smoker & Apr 1995 & End of study & Dec 2014 & 19.75 \\ \hline
Non-Smoker & Jan 1995 & Lung cancer & Aug 2000 & 5.67 \\ \hline
Smoker & Feb 1995 & Lung cancer & Jul 2002 & 7.50 \\ \hline
Smoker & Jun 1995 & End of study & Dec 2014 & 19.58 \\ \hline
Smoker & Mar 1995 & Death & Nov 2001 & 6.75 \\ \hline
Non-Smoker & Jan 1995 & End of study & Dec 2014 & 20.00 \\ \hline
Non-Smoker & Mar 1995 & End of study & Dec 2014 & 19.83 \\ \hline
Smoker & Jan 1995 & Lung cancer & Feb 2012 & 17.17 \\ \hline
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Person-time example calculated}
\label{tab:example-person-time-calc}
\end{table}
The first row gives 11.25 person-years because you just take the difference between March 2006 and January 1995 which would be 11 years and 3 months or 11.25. The rates are as follows,
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rate in exposed, 
        $\!\begin{aligned}[t]
            r_e &= \dfrac{\text{Total smokers with lung cancer}}{\text{Total person-years from all smokers}} \\[1.5ex]
                &= \dfrac{3}{11.25 + 7.50 + 19.58 + 6.75 + 17.17} \\[1.5ex]
                &\approx 0.0482 \text{ or 4.82 per 100 person-years}
        \end{aligned}$ 
    \item Rate in non-exposed,
        $\!\begin{aligned}[t]
            r_0 &= \dfrac{\text{Total non-smokers with lung cancer}}{\text{Total person-years from all non-smokers}} \\[1.5ex]
                &= \dfrac{1}{19.42 + 19.75 + 5.67 + 20.00 + 19.83} \\[1.5ex]
                &\approx 0.0118 \text{ or 1.18 per 100 person}
        \end{aligned}$ 
    \item Rate ratio = $\dfrac{4.82 \text{ per 100 person-years}}{1.18 \text{ per 100 person-years}} = 4.08$
\end{itemize}
